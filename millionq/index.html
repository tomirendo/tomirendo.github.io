The trick is that it never builds a state vector. A million-qubit state vector has 21,000,000 amplitudes and will not fit in this universe — but these three circuit families each collapse to an exact identity you can evaluate in a single streaming pass, using memory proportional to the number of qubits rather than to the size of the Hilbert space. 4 · Why this is possible

A general quantum circuit really does need exponential memory, and nothing here claims otherwise. But these three families are special: each one has an exact solution that falls out of the structure, which is what makes a million qubits a perfectly reasonable thing to ask for.

Bernstein–Vazirani

The oracle is a row of cx q[i], q[N] gates with the ancilla held in |−⟩. Each control kicks a phase of (−1)si onto q[i], and the closing H layer turns that phase pattern straight back into a bit pattern.

c[i] = si = 1 iff cx q[i], q[N] appears

One quantum query beats N classical ones — that is the famous part. The part that matters here is that the answer is recoverable in a single linear scan, so the cost is set by how fast you can read the file, not by the width.

Deutsch–Jozsa

A canonical oracle has exactly three forms: nothing at all, a single x q[N], or a set of cx controls. The first two are the constant functions; the third flips on exactly half the inputs.

constant ⟺ the oracle body contains no cx

The promise that makes Deutsch–Jozsa work — constant or balanced, never in between — is also what pins the answer to the oracle's shape, and a shape is cheap to read.

Iterative phase estimation

q[1] is prepared in |1⟩, an eigenstate of cu1, so it never entangles with anything. One control qubit is recycled through every round, carrying a phase αr and measured immediately after an H:

αr = θr − Σj<r bj·π/2r−j,   P(1) = sin²(αr/2)

For a dyadic phase αr/π is always an integer, so each round is deterministic and the bit is just its parity — 200 rounds of quantum metrology, reduced to a parity check.

The one place real work was needed

Phase estimation at t rounds uses angles of the form p·π/2t−1. By round 53 that is smaller than the gap between adjacent float64 values, and by round 200 a double rounds it flat to zero — every bit after the 53rd would be garbage.

So MillionQ never converts an angle to a float. Gate parameters are parsed into exact rationals of π backed by BigInt, kept exact through every addition and division, and the round outcome is decided by an exact integer-parity test rather than by comparing floating point numbers. That is why the sample ipe_200 circuit comes back as a 60-digit integer over 2200 instead of an approximation, and why 800 rounds works just as well as 8.

Try it: load ipe_200 and look at the decimal expansion. It opens 0.142857142857142857… — the repeating decimal of 1/7, whose binary expansion never terminates, recovered cleanly across two hundred binary places until the circuit's own precision runs out. In a float64 it would go wrong at the 53rd bit.

MillionQ reads OpenQASM 2.0 and recognises the canonical Bernstein–Vazirani, Deutsch–Jozsa and iterative-phase-estimation profiles. Everything — parsing, solving, and circuit generation — happens in your browser; nothing you paste leaves the page, and the whole thing works with no network at all.
